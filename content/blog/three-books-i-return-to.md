I buy more books than I read and reread almost none of them. These three are the exceptions, the ones that have earned a second and third pass.

The first is The Design of Everyday Things. It's about doors and kettles more than screens, which is why it lasts. Once you've read it you can't stop seeing affordances and bad signifiers everywhere, and you start asking whether a thing is confusing because the user is wrong or because you are. Usually it's you.

The second is a slim one, Bird by Bird. It's pitched at writers but it's really about finishing anything. The bit everyone quotes is "short assignments", doing the small piece in front of you instead of panicking about the whole. I think about it as much over a stuck feature as over a stuck paragraph.

The third changes. Right now it's a book of John Berger essays that I keep open on the desk and read a page of when I'm stuck. It's not a reference and it doesn't help directly. It just resets how I'm looking at things, which is sometimes the only thing that's actually wrong.

That's the test, I think. Not whether a book taught me something the first time, but whether it's still useful the third.
