You've seen the headline: a handful of chatbot questions costs a bottle of water. The figure comes from real research, but it's doing a lot of quiet work. It depends on which data centre, which season, how the local grid makes its power, and whether you count the training of the model or just the asking. The honest version is a range so wide it's nearly a shrug, and anyone quoting a single tidy number is selling you a feeling.

That cuts both ways. The footprint is real and it's climbing fast, training especially. Dismissing it because the stats are messy is as lazy as panicking over one of them.

What's missing from most of these pieces is a sense of scale. Most of the internet's traffic is video. An evening of background streaming, autoplaying feeds, and things left buffering in a tab, multiplied across a few billion phones, is an enormous standing cost that almost nobody frames as a crisis. We made our peace with it because it arrived slowly. AI arrived loudly, so it gets the water-bottle headline.

I'm not arguing AI is fine and you should relax. I'm arguing the useful question isn't "is this bad", it's "what did this particular thing cost, and was it worth it". A query that saves an hour is a different trade from a generated video nobody asked for, the same way a video call home is a different trade from leaving a livestream on overnight.

And the biggest lever isn't any of us using less. It's what powers the buildings. A data centre on clean power and one on coal do the same work for wildly different cost. That's the number worth shouting about, and it's the one that rarely makes the headline.
